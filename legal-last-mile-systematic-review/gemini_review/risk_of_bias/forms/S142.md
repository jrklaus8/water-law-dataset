## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Allaire et al. (2024) present an ambitious, large-scale analytical cross-sectional spatial regression study of 31,383 Census block groups across nine eastern U.S. states examining how municipal incorporation status (Incorporated vs. Unincorporated Island, Fringe, and Outlying communities) and high poverty (>=20%) relate to the proportion of community land area unserved by centralized sewer and drinking water systems. Methodological strengths include a transparent geospatial protocol using building footprints to capture unincorporated settlements overlooked by Census Designated Places (69% of unincorporated community land), extensive public-records collection to represent 96% of community water systems and 94% of publicly owned treatment works, careful remediation of EPA SDWIS mailing-address geocoding errors, filtering of unreliable ACS poverty estimates (CV <= 40%), and multilevel negative binomial regression with an area offset and county/state random intercepts. Overall methodological concern is rated as 'Some concern' under the JBI Analytical Cross-Sectional Checklist due to three primary limitations: (1) heterogeneous outcome measurement and potential differential misclassification, notably the use of municipal boundaries as proxies for missing utility service boundaries (Tier 2b, e.g., 28% of Mississippi POTWs, which mechanically aligns measured service boundaries with municipal incorporation status), circular buffer approximations for smaller utilities (Tier 3, >1/3 of systems in several states), omission of unlocated minor utilities, deterministic parcel imputations in Florida, and temporal misalignment across 2010 Census geographies, 2018 building footprints, and multi-year utility records; (2) residual confounding and reverse causality, as regression models omit block-group racial/ethnic composition (despite racialized municipal underbounding being a core theoretical mechanism) and cross-sectional associations cannot separate exclusionary governance from state laws (e.g., in Florida and Texas) requiring water/sewer infrastructure prior to annexation; and (3) unmodeled fine-scale spatial autocorrelation across adjacent block groups and majority-area assignment of mixed-jurisdiction block groups.
