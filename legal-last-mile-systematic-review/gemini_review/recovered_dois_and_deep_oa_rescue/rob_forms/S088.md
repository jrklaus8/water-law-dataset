## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Otsuki (2016) is a primary qualitative ethnographic case study examining how community-managed bio-centre sanitation facilities in Kibera, Nairobi become embedded in local place-making and require ongoing co-production with municipal state actors. Appraised with the CASP Qualitative Checklist, the study exhibits clear research aims (Q1: Yes), an appropriate qualitative ethnographic design (Q2: Yes; Q3: Yes), and a coherent statement of findings (Q9: Yes) with strong substantive value for understanding administrative and operational 'last mile' barriers in informal settlements (Q10). However, it raises moderate methodological concern overall (3 'Can't tell' and 2 'No' ratings across Q1-Q9). Specifically, recruitment criteria for selecting eight bio-centres (out of ~50) and individual interviewees/users via the partnering NGO are insufficiently detailed (Q4: Can't tell); data collection procedures (interview guides, recording/note-taking, translation, and a timeline discrepancy between 2010 interview endnotes and the stated 2011-2013 fieldwork window) are under-reported (Q5: Can't tell); qualitative coding and analytic rigour are not described (Q8: Can't tell); researcher-participant power relations stemming from the author's UNU role and close partnership with the implementing NGO (Umande Trust) are not critically examined (Q6: No); and research ethics/consent safeguards are absent despite naming a specific youth CBO alongside a second-hand allegation of drug dealing (Q7: No).
