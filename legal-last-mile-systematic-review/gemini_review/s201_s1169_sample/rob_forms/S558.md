## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Selfa, Bain, and Moreno (2014) present a primary qualitative case study examining how Colombia's political economy of land and water governance shapes the implementation and limits of Bonsucro sustainability certification in the Valle del Cauca sugarcane-ethanol sector. Because all quantitative figures in the paper (e.g., land Gini/ownership shares, CVC surface and groundwater concession shares, river nutrient loads) are cited from secondary sources rather than original quantitative data collection or analysis, the CASP Qualitative Checklist is the appropriate appraisal instrument. Methodological strengths include clearly articulated theoretical aims grounded in Ribot and Peluso's theory of access (Q1-Q3), detailed documentary scrutiny of Bonsucro Version 3.0 Principles 1 and 5 alongside national and regional water regulations (Decree 1541/78, Agreement 042/2010, Law 99 of 1993), and balanced presentation of contrasting narratives from industry associations (Asocana, Procana), critics, and rural residents (Q9-Q10). However, methodological reporting is sparse across core empirical procedures: recruitment mechanics and group-level sample counts for the 14 exploratory interviews (and their relation to rural community residents cited on p. 465) are unspecified (Q4: Can't tell); interview protocols, recording/transcription, and translation procedures are omitted (Q5: Can't tell); researcher reflexivity is absent (Q6: No); formal ethics/consent procedures are unreported despite sensitive conflict/corruption topics (Q7: Can't tell); and qualitative coding/analytic methods are not described (Q8: Can't tell). Moreover, because Bonsucro certification in Colombia was still in pre-audit and standard-negotiation stages in 2012, conclusions about certification reinforcing water and land grabs are partly prospective and cannot isolate Bonsucro's effect from long-standing state policies and CVC governance.
