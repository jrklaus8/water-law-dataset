## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Structured non-validated judgment (Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework; no numeric composite score): Graham (2006) is a legal-historical and institutional policy case study examining how utility privatisation (1989-1990), independent regulation (Ofwat, Offer/Ofgas/Ofgem), judicial review, and post-1997 statutory reforms shaped low-income household access to water and electricity in Great Britain. Methodologically, its primary strengths lie in legal source accuracy (D1: strong), jurisdictional specificity distinguishing England and Wales water law from Scotland and Northern Ireland (D2: strong), and rich institutional contextualization of the 'last mile' of domestic water billing, metering on council estates, municipal judicial review against Budget Payment Units (R v Director General of Water Services ex parte Oldham Borough Council, 1998), and the resulting statutory ban on domestic water disconnections and water-limiting devices under the Water Industry Act 1999 (D10: strong). Conversely, as a source of causal empirical evidence on consumer welfare outcomes, the study exhibits substantial limitations: secondary reports and isolated administrative statistics are selected narratively without a transparent search or sampling protocol (D5, D6: weak), several empirical figures lack complete bibliographic citations or denominators while pre-payment meter 'self-disconnection' goes unrecorded in official disconnection statistics (D7, D11: weak), and the central claim that privatisation and regulation did not worsen disadvantaged consumers' material position rests on descriptive historical comparison without counterfactual identification or empirical control for acknowledged macro-level confounders such as world oil prices, European Community capital mandates, and welfare transfers (D8, D9: weak).
