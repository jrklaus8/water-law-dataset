## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Johnson et al. (2004) present a single-town mixed-methods case study of Mebane, North Carolina, integrating descriptive GIS spatial mapping of 2000 Census demographics, municipal/Extraterritorial Jurisdiction (ETJ) boundaries, sewer lines, and proposed NC 119 bypass corridors with qualitative participant observation, city council minutes (1991-2002), and personal communications. Appraised with the MMAT (Qualitative 1.1-1.5, Quantitative descriptive 4.1-4.5, and Mixed Methods 5.1-5.5), the study's core strength is its compelling spatial-institutional integration (5.1-5.3 rated Yes): superimposing geo-coded corporate limits, ETJ boundaries, and sewer infrastructure onto neighborhood racial composition vividly demonstrates municipal underbounding, whereby historic African American neighborhoods (West End, White Level, Buckhorn/Perry Hill) are regulated under Mebane's ETJ while excluded from annexation, municipal voting rights, and sewer service—even adjacent to the town's wastewater treatment plant and upscale White subdivisions. However, per MMAT 2018 guidance that overall methodological quality cannot exceed the weakest component, the study raises high methodological concern (5.5 rated No; 1.2-1.5, 4.4-4.5, and 5.4 rated Can't tell). The article contains no standalone Methods section; it provides no sampling frame, observation schedule, coding protocol, or reflexivity statement for its qualitative 'participant observation' and 'content analysis of town council deliberations'; it omits technical GIS documentation (including attributing 'block-level race data' to Census 2000 Summary File 3 and leaving the 87% bypass property-loss calculation unexplained); it lacks primary household septic survey data because residents feared property condemnation (relying instead on a brief summary of a secondary 2002 EPA water-sampling report); and it makes strong causal claims of discriminatory intent without formal statistical or engineering controls for infrastructure cost, density, or topography.
