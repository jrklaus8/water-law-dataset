## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Biddle and Baehler (2019) conduct an ex post comparative institutional and legal-historical case study of two U.S. municipal drinking water systems—New York City (1900-1975 and 1976-present) and Flint, Michigan (1950-2012 and 2013-present)—using qualitative process tracing grounded in publicly available government inquiry reports, utility documents, FOIA emails, news coverage, law reviews, and secondary histories. Appraised under the Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework (as a structured non-validated judgment, with no numeric composite score), the study exhibits strong conceptual operationalization of polycentricity (separating formal structural presence from adaptive/maladaptive function across six enabling conditions in Tables 1-2), strong jurisdictional and historical specificity, and rich institutional contextualization of U.S. Safe Drinking Water Act federalism, EPA Filtration Avoidance Determinations, the 1997 NYC Watershed Memorandum of Agreement, and Michigan's Emergency Manager receivership regime. Independent coding by two researchers with consensus reconciliation and candid methodological reflexivity further support credibility. However, internal validity for causal inference is inherently constrained by purposive selection on extreme poles of the outcome continuum (NYC vs Flint), non-systematic documentary source compilation with reliance on secondary/journalistic accounts rather than primary statutory citations, and the absence of counterfactual controls to isolate governance norms from major co-evolving confounders such as municipal wealth disparities, deindustrialization, power asymmetries, and leadership agency.
