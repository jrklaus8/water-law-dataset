## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

This paper (an English translation in Simbiótica 2026 of an article originally published in Saúde e Sociedade in 2020) is a political-historical and doctrinal-empirical hybrid study examining environmental and institutional racism in Brazilian sanitation and health from the colonial period to contemporary constitutional governance. Because it combines doctrinal/policy and historical synthesis ('exploratory-level bibliographic research according to Gil, 2009') with descriptive secondary tabulations of national administrative and census databases (IBGE 2010 Demographic Census housing/sanitation deficits and Tabnet DataSUS 1996-2014 SIM mortality and 2008-2016 SIH hospitalizations for Diseases Related to Inadequate Environmental Sanitation [DRSAI]), it fits the Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework rather than a systematic review tool (AMSTAR 2), a primary qualitative fieldwork checklist (CASP), or an individual-level analytical cross-sectional regression checklist (JBI). Under a structured non-validated judgment, the study demonstrates strong legal source accuracy (1988 Federal Constitution arts. 196, 200, 225; Law 11.445/2007; Law 12.288/2010; PNSIPN 2013), jurisdictional specificity, rich historical-institutional contextualization, explicit DRSAI and Census outcome definitions, and unusually candid researcher reflexivity. Conversely, it exhibits weak causal identification, confounding control, selection process, and coding transparency: bibliographic selection is non-systematic, quantitative analyses present unadjusted national compositional shares without age, income, urban/rural, or regional standardization, 23% of DataSUS DRSAI death records lack race/color identification, Indigenous and Asian-descent groups are omitted from comparative charts, and exact ICD-10 and IBGE variable coding scripts are not archived. As the author explicitly acknowledges in the Final Considerations (PDF p. 18), the analysis serves as a macro-level exploratory framing calling for deeper local/comparative quantitative and qualitative research rather than a causally identified policy evaluation.
