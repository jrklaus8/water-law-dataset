## 2. Cross-Tool Summary Domains (`CODEBOOK.md` §8)

| Cross-Tool Field | Judgment |
|---|---|
| `selection_bias` | High concern: The study frame is restricted to NCWSC piped water billing itineraries, systematically excluding informal and newly settled residential neighbourhoods outside the municipal piped network, and additionally excludes 554 itineraries (including 92 residential itineraries) lacking geographical coordinates without comparing included versus excluded areas. |
| `measurement_bias` | High concern: Outcome per-capita domestic consumption divides NCWSC billed meter volumes by WorldPop random-forest modelled grid populations, omitting 29% non-revenue water (including illegal connections, leaks, and irregular/unmetered connections), omitting alternative water sources (vendors, boreholes, tankers), and conflating residential economic/commercial water use with domestic consumption. Exposure zoning categories rely on coarse built-environment/land-use proxies with an abrupt 2013 population shift between R1 and R4. |
| `confounding` | High concern: The multivariable negative binomial mixed-effects model adjusts for only three ecological covariates (residential zoning category, built-up settlement age, and modelled itinerary population, plus a random effect for year) due to unavailable small-area census statistics. Unmeasured household socio-economic heterogeneity, commercial/productive water use within residential connections, private water storage capacity, and off-grid water sources create substantial residual confounding and ecological fallacy risk. |
| `attrition` | Moderate concern / Unclear: 554 itineraries (including 92 residential itineraries) were dropped at the outset due to missing geographical coordinates; reservoir water production metering was only available from November 2016 to May 2018 due to meter breakages; rationing schedules were restricted to October 2016-December 2018; and the completeness of monthly customer meter readings across the 132 months (2008-2018) per itinerary is not reported. |
| `reporting_bias` | Some concern: No pre-registered protocol or pre-analysis plan is reported, and multivariable covariate selection relied on univariable p < 0.2 screening. Several internal numerical inconsistencies appear in the published text and tables, including mismatched itinerary filtering counts/percentages (PDF Page 5), a reversed inequality sign for 2018 R4 density in Table 2 (<18,000), low-income built-up subgroup counts summing to 263 versus 252 total in Table 4, and an incompatible p-value (0.425) for a rate ratio of 0.54 (95% CI: 0.53-0.56) in Table 4. |
| `legal_measurement_quality` | Adequate: Official administrative residential zoning classifications (R1-R4 derived from the 1973 Master Plan / 1985 water distribution plans and updated via the 2014 Nairobi Integrated Urban Development Master Plan), NCWSC domestic block tariffs (2015-2018), and formal weekly rationing schedules (2016-2018) are sourced directly from utility and ministry records and cross-validated against KSCBS tenure/slum indicators, though informal land-use transitions, an unexplained sharp 2013 reclassification shift, and unenforced private kiosk retail pricing introduce administrative proxy error. |
| `outcome_measurement_quality` | Weak (high risk of measurement error): While water sufficiency uses Gleick's standard >1500 L/capita/month benchmark, numerator data come solely from billed utility customer meters (missing unbilled/illegal consumption within the 29% non-revenue water loss and all non-piped water sources, while including small-scale commercial uses) and denominator data rely on dasymetric gridded population projections that include unpiped residents within itinerary boundaries and mask intra-itinerary household inequality. |

## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

High concern overall under the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross Sectional Studies (no numeric score computed). Mutono et al. (2022) assemble an informative 11-year spatially disaggregated utility billing dataset across 2,380 residential itineraries in Nairobi (2008-2018), clearly describe Nairobi's water supply and tariff/rationing architecture, apply an objective domestic sufficiency threshold (>1500 L/capita/month), and cross-validate utility residential land-use classes against 1,137 households in the 2012-2013 KSCBS. However, substantial methodological risks limit internal validity: (1) selection bias from excluding off-grid informal settlements altogether and dropping 554 itineraries (92 residential) lacking geolocation; (2) outcome measurement bias from dividing NCWSC billed meter volumes (which miss 29% non-revenue water including illegal/irregular meters, omit off-grid/vended water sources, and include non-domestic commercial uses) by modelled WorldPop grid populations; (3) ecological fallacy and unmeasured confounding in a multivariable model restricted to three ecological covariates; and (4) unaddressed spatial residual autocorrelation (p = 0.028) and internal numerical/statistical inconsistencies in Table 4 and sample filtering counts.
