## 3. Overall Narrative Summary

Cortes et al. (2021) present a three-country comparative legal-institutional case study (Brazil, Colombia, and Peru) examining why national constitutional amendments to enshrine a freestanding right to piped water succeeded in Peru (2017) but stalled or failed in Brazil and Colombia between 2010 and 2019/2021. Appraised under the Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework (structured non-validated judgment), the study is strong in primary statutory/constitutional citation accuracy (D1), jurisdictional specificity across unitary and federal regimes (D2), and nuanced institutional contextualization of water regulators, public-private utilities, and judicial enforcement writs such as Colombia's tutela versus Peru's slower amparo (D10). However, methodological transparency and empirical rigor are weak across sampling, selection, measurement, coding, causal identification, and replication domains (D5-D9, D11-D12), with researcher reflexivity not assessable (D13). In particular, selection of the 21 constitutional bills, 12 laws, and 5 regulatory rules lacks explicit screening criteria or complete docket enumeration; no content-analysis codebook or rubric is provided for cross-country classifications; human-subjects interviews are disclosed in the IRB and Data Availability statements (pp. 14-15) and referenced on p. 11 without any methodological description in Section 3; small-N comparison (N = 3) cannot rule out political-economy confounders; and the study evaluates only formal constitutional amendment outcomes rather than empirical water-access outcomes.
