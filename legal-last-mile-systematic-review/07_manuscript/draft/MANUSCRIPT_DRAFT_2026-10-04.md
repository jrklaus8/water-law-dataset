## Abstract (structured; draft)

**Background.** Physical expansion of water and sanitation infrastructure does not automatically guarantee household connection or effective use where legal, regulatory, and administrative conditions—such as eligibility screening, tenure and documentation rules, tariffs, bureaucratic discretion, and enforcement—operate as barriers at the 'legal last mile.'

**Objective.** To map and synthesise the empirical evidence on how legal and administrative institutions shape the translation of physical availability of water and sanitation infrastructure into effective household access, and to synthesise quantitative evidence where it is comparable.

**Methods.** An AI-assisted systematic review: 34,594 records from multiple databases (27,481 after deduplication) were screened at title/abstract level and 2,277 full texts assessed; 1,080 reports (representing 1,078 distinct studies; 1,076 counting partial sample overlaps) were included and extracted into a 1,080-row database, and appraised with the instrument matching each design (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, JBI, MMAT, CASP, AMSTAR 2, and a project-specific non-validated framework for legal-documentary studies). Quantitative findings were synthesised without meta-analysis (SWiM). The protocol was not registered in advance; most steps were carried out by a large language model with partial human verification.

**Results.** 1,080 included reports (1,078 distinct studies; 85% since 2010) spanned many countries (India, Brazil, South Africa, the United States and Ghana the most frequent), but only 63 (6%) used designs able to support a causal claim. 60 studies had an extractable effect-size row; none could be pooled. In three SWiM families the direction of association was mostly favourable for legal-status, tenure and recognition mechanisms (Family A, k = 18), uniformly favourable or mixed for assistance, participation, trust and external-support mechanisms (Family B, k = 6), and without a dominant direction for ownership, regulation, institutional-capacity and administrative-structure mechanisms (Family C, k = 20); the families are thematic mechanism groupings, not homogeneous exposure–outcome pairs. The author independently confirmed 197 chronological full-text inclusions (S001–S200 net of 3 later retirements) and adjudicated a further 248 full-text records (164 exclusions, 84 inclusions) in the scope, consistency and audit passes, so 445 of the 2,277 full-text decisions (19.5%) carry human verification or adjudication; no extracted value has been independently verified.

**Conclusions.** The empirical literature extensively documents associations between legal and administrative conditions and household water and sanitation access across diverse institutional contexts, but rarely employs designs capable of isolating causal effects and does not support a single pooled meta-analytic estimate. Overall certainty for causal or cross-jurisdictional magnitude claims remains low to very low, underscoring the need for quasi-experimental and comparative socio-legal designs targeting specific administrative rules.

**Registration.** Not registered before the work was done. The protocol was fixed in writing on 2026-08-22 (see the Repository Audit Trail, Supplementary File S3), before any database was searched, and was registered retrospectively on OSF, frozen at release `0.2.0-interim` (2026-10-14) [author: add the OSF identifier and submission date once submitted, and label the registration retrospective].

## 1. Introduction

Access to water and sanitation is usually measured by infrastructure coverage, but a household within reach of a network may still be unable to connect, or to keep and use a connection, because of legal and administrative conditions: who is recognised as eligible, what documents or tenure the application requires, what it costs, how much discretion officials hold, and how rules are enforced or reviewed (Bakker et al., 2008; Criqui, 2015; Hackenbroch & Hossain, 2012; Olmstead, 2004). This review calls the gap between physical availability and effective access the *legal last mile*. The term organises the question; it is not assumed to be established by the evidence (Supplementary File S4 §14).

The relevant evidence is scattered across disciplines and designs — public administration, economics, law, geography, public health, development studies — and across mechanisms and outcome measures that are rarely comparable. Most of the systematic reviews in the corpus synthesise a single intervention type or governance model: community participation (Nelson et al., 2021), behaviour-change and legal-provision approaches to household sewer connection (Alam et al., 2025), or the privatisation of one city's water services (Hendraputra, 2026). The closest cross-cutting review in the corpus, a scoping review of access inequalities in slums, organises its 33 studies into five barrier themes, one of which is governance and policy, rather than by the legal and administrative mechanisms through which access is conditioned (Abdulhadi et al., 2024). To the authors' knowledge, no prior synthesis maps this evidence with a mechanism-based framework.

**Objectives.** (1) Describe the empirical evidence on legal and administrative conditions and access to water and sanitation, by design, setting, mechanism and outcome. (2) Synthesise quantitative evidence where exposure, comparator, outcome and effect measure are comparable enough to do so defensibly, and otherwise report the direction of association transparently. (3) State what the evidence base does and does not allow us to conclude, including how reliable the review's own process is.

### 1.1 Conceptual framework

Structural conditions (legal and property status, documentation, income, geography, institutional capacity) act through an administrative architecture (eligibility screening, administrative burden, discretion, accommodation, enforcement, review, participation) and administrative navigation (understanding requirements, applying, satisfying requirements, obtaining accommodation, challenging decisions) to produce service access (formal connection, continuity, reliability, quantity, affordability, effective use) and, ultimately, inclusion or exclusion (Supplementary File S4 §5). Administrative law is the central lens, not the only candidate cause; property, planning, municipal, utility-regulatory and political institutions are in scope.

### 2.2 Research questions and eligibility

The primary question asked how legal and administrative institutions shape the translation of physical availability of water and sanitation infrastructure into effective household access, and what evidence exists on the mechanisms by which eligibility screening, administrative burden, discretion, accommodation and enforcement produce or mitigate exclusion. The secondary question asked, where evidence is sufficiently comparable, for the magnitude of association between specific legal or administrative conditions and access outcomes. Studies were eligible if they examined water or sanitation access, examined a legal, administrative, institutional, regulatory or governance factor, and contained empirical evidence or a systematic empirical synthesis with an access-relevant outcome and an identifiable design; English, Portuguese and Dutch were eligible without translation (Supplementary File S8). Qualitative socio-legal studies were not excluded for being unpoolable. **Scope reading of criterion 2 (post-hoc).** Criterion 2 is literally broad. After a blind re-read by two other AI models disagreed with the AI on about half of the full-text exclusions they re-read (34 of 70 for the primary model; the author's own adjudication of the 52 exclusions flagged by either model then confirmed 49 and reversed 3, Section 5.2), the author ruled on 2026-10-13 that it would be applied as a legal or administrative access rule or mechanism (eligibility screening, administrative burden, documentation or tenure requirements, tariff or cost-recovery rules, discretion or accommodation, enforcement, or utility ownership or regulatory structure directly governing service access) rather than as project delivery, behavioural promotion or unanalysed macro-governance background, and to included and excluded studies alike. Because this was decided after the disagreement was seen, it is a post-hoc interpretive clarification of unchanged wording, not an a priori rule. It was followed on 2026-10-13 and 14 by consistency passes over the included studies applying criterion 3 (no primary data and no systematic method, code E05), criterion 4 (the outcome is not a household access outcome, E04) and the exposure test (E03); in these passes 84 included reports were moved to the exclusions and 3 exclusions to the inclusions. Eligibility never depends on the significance, magnitude or direction of a study's estimate (clarification of 2026-10-14).

### 2.3 Search and selection

Searches closed on 2026-09-11; SSRN and Westlaw/Lexis were not searched (Supplementary File S9, README Known limitations). Selection was by an AI first pass (title/abstract and full text) with written rationales and standardised exclusion codes (E01–E12); the human reviewer (the author) covered the 3,665 title/abstract records the AI marked include or unsure, and confirmed 197 of the 1,080 full-text includes; the author then adjudicated 248 further full-text decisions, so 445 of the 2,277 decisions (19.5%) carry human verification or adjudication. Full-text retrieval was closed by the author's decision on 2026-09-28 with 1,382 title/abstract includes never assessed; the unassessed set is older on average (see 3.1). The 1,080 included items are reports, describing 1,078 distinct studies on the definite same-data links (1,076 counting the partial overlaps); no companion report was collapsed.

### 2.4 Data extraction

A 92-field extraction form (Supplementary File S10) was completed for every included study by the AI, largely from full text; 10 studies were extracted from abstract or metadata only and an audit found further rows with signs of shallow extraction (Limitations). The AI later re-read 127 studies from full texts found in the author's Drive; of the 76 rows flagged only by the sparse-record audit, 9 had an abstract-level extraction that was materially wrong or incomplete (Supplementary File S11). There was no second extractor. A seeded sample for human second extraction has been prepared (Supplementary File S12) but not yet completed.

### 2.5 Risk of bias and critical appraisal

Each study was appraised with the instrument matching its design: RoB 2 (cluster variant) for randomised trials (5 reports of 4 distinct trials), ROBINS-I (58), JBI cross-sectional (130), MMAT (195), CASP qualitative (248) and AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews (18). For 426 legal-documentary or doctrinal studies no published instrument fits, and this project's own **non-validated** Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework was used; no study was judged not appraisable. Ratings were assigned by rule from the extracted fields, not by signalling-question reading of each paper, except for the studies re-appraised from full text (listed in Supplementary File S7). Ratings therefore describe the extraction as much as the paper.

### 2.6 Synthesis

Each study was coded to one of nine mechanism families (eligibility, status and documentation; procedural burden; fees, tariffs and subsidies; discretion and accommodation; enforcement and sanctions; review and redress; institutional structure and coordination; participation and assistance; regulatory model and ownership) or to `multiple`, and to one of four outcome families (primary connection, effective access, economic access, administrative outcome) or `multiple`; the families are not pooled with each other. The labels were mapped from the free-text labels of the extraction by keyword rules, with the rows the rules could not place resolved by the AI under the author's review (four studies the mapping could not place were excluded), and the original labels are preserved; the mapping is mechanical and unvalidated, and the author has not checked it. Free-text study-design labels were mapped to the design list (eight values plus `other`) in the same way. A feasibility judgment (Supplementary File S13) concluded that no family reaches the bar for meta-analysis, so 60 effect-size rows were compiled into three structured syntheses reporting direction and significance of each study's own estimate (SWiM). The family assignments were made by judgement and organise the write-up only: on a keyword reading of the exposure definitions, 28 of 44 family-assigned rows sit outside the wording of the family they were placed in (Supplementary File S14), so the families are loose groupings, not tests of the specified constructs. Families A, B and C are therefore reported as thematic mechanism groupings used to organise structured vote counting (SWiM), not as homogeneous exposure–outcome pairs, and their headings describe the clusters actually synthesised (legal status, tenure, recognition and governance arrangements; assistance, participation, trust and external support; ownership, regulation, institutional capacity and administrative structure), which is also why pooling was rejected. No effect was converted to a common scale. Sensitivity analyses removed abstract-only and shallow extractions, linked reports and two coding judgment calls, and would remove the rows of studies not flagged eligible (none remain since S589's row was taken out on 2026-10-14); they are reported as a supplementary table (Supplementary File S15), a standing pre-publication requirement, and the direction coding itself still awaits human verification on a sample.

### 3.7 Gaps in the evidence base

- **Geographic:** the Netherlands (1 single-country study) and Canada (15) — two of the dissertation's three comparison jurisdictions — are barely represented against Brazil (80); 555 (51%) of studies sit in the ten commonest single countries.
- **Design:** 5 randomised trials (4 distinct) and 58 quasi-experimental studies against 426 (39%) studies appraised with the project's own non-validated framework (the residual category for designs no published instrument fits, including doctrinal and documentary studies).
- **Mechanisms/outcomes:** redress and review mechanisms and application-process outcomes are thinly coded (see Section 3.3); sanitation access less than water.
- **Retrieval:** 1,382 title/abstract includes were never assessed. Their median publication year is 2014 versus 2018 for assessed records; 8.9% were published before 2000 versus 3.0%, and 30.8% in 2020 or later versus 42.7% — the unassessed set is older, so the corpus under-represents earlier literature.

## 4. Discussion

*This section is interpretation. Each point rests on the numbers cited in Section 3 and could be wrong.*

1. **The literature documents co-occurrence far more than causation.** With 63 (6%) causal-capable designs (the mechanism-certainty code now follows the design, Section 3.2), the base can say that legal/administrative features and access outcomes are associated across many settings, but rarely that one causes the other. *Rests on:* Section 3.2 counts. *Would change if:* the 1,382 unassessed records or future searches contain many more designs with credible identification.
2. **Recognition and formal status tend to go with better access (Family A), but the pattern is thin.** 13 of 18 Favourable, 3 Unfavourable (S104 tenure and interruptions; S1020 a watershed programme and collection time; S1121 water-adequacy screening and permitting): S104 appraised with JBI Cross-Sectional; S1020 appraised with ROBINS-I; S1121 appraised with ROBINS-I. *Rests on:* the SWiM coding, a judgment by the same AI. Not a pooled estimate and not certainty-graded.
3. **Assistance and trust mechanisms look uniformly favourable (Family B) — but on six studies with six different mechanisms.** The consistency may reflect small numbers or reporting of positive findings; nothing here excludes either.
4. **Ownership and price: private ownership goes with worse affordability in the three studies (S526, S539, S749), but the two US samples probably overlap**, so this may be two independent samples, not three (Supplementary File S36 LR09, an audit inference).
5. **Most institutional analysis in this corpus is descriptive-qualitative and concentrated on fragmentation, discretion and fees.** This may reflect what the search and the closed retrieval surfaced (English-language, post-2010, database-indexed) as much as what the world contains.
6. **The dissertation's comparative frame (Netherlands, Canada, Brazil) cannot be tested from this base as it stands**: one Dutch and 15 Canadian single-country studies.

## 5. Limitations

- **Incomplete full-text screening.** 1,382 of 3,659 records (37.8%) were not assessed, and those not assessed are older on average; the review is not exhaustive and may under-represent earlier literature.
- **AI-conducted judgments with partial verification.** Screening decisions, extraction, appraisal and coding of direction of association were made by the same AI system. The author confirmed 197 full-text includes (S001–S200) and adjudicated 248 further full-text decisions, so 445 of the 2,277 decisions (19.5%) carry human verification or adjudication (281 includes and 164 excludes); 73 decided rows had no reviewer recorded at the time (the label was inferred afterwards, and the author has since ruled on all of them), and the title/abstract exclusions were not human-checked. A blind re-read of 70 full-text exclusions with an available PDF (62 of them a random sample stratified by exclusion code) by two non-Claude models (Codex, Gemini) flagged 52; the author adjudicated all 52, confirming 49 exclusions and reversing 3 to inclusion (S1165, S1166, S1167). That is 3 of the 62 sampled exclusions (4.8%; 95% Wilson interval 1.7–13.3%), or about 50 of the 1,038 content-based exclusions if the rate held (interval 17–138; about 37 when scaled within exclusion code). It counts only the exclusions the models flagged and rests on the narrow reading of eligibility criterion 2 adopted after the disagreement was seen, so it is an estimate and not a verified count; it replaces an earlier unadjudicated extrapolation that took the models' verdicts at face value.
- **Thin extraction for some studies.** 10 studies were extracted from abstract or metadata only; there was no second extractor. The AI re-read 127 studies from full texts after the first extraction (an AI check of AI work, not independent verification); in 9 of the 76 sparse-audit rows among them the abstract-level extraction was materially wrong or incomplete (Supplementary File S11).
- **Appraisal validity.** Ratings are rule-based; 426 studies were appraised with a non-validated project instrument; 51 of 130 JBI ratings are 'high concern' largely reflecting sparse extraction; all 18 AMSTAR 2 reviews received a confidence rating (17 Critically Low and 1 Low).
- **No pooled estimate.** Direction-of-association syntheses rest on 60 effect-size rows and are neither effect estimates nor certainty-graded (GRADE).
- **Classifications.** Mechanism, outcome and study-design labels were mapped to controlled vocabularies by keyword rules on 2026-10-14 (original labels preserved); the mapping is mechanical and unvalidated, and country and legal-system fields remain free text.
- **A post-hoc scope reading.** Eligibility criterion 2 was applied under a narrow, rule-type reading adopted on 2026-10-13, after an AI-versus-AI disagreement about the exclusions had been seen. The adjudication and consistency passes that followed moved 84 included reports to the exclusions (the 4 inclusions that the models had also questioned, all excluded by the author, and 80 others) and 3 exclusions to the inclusions. It is a post-hoc clarification, not an a priori rule; the wording of the criterion is unchanged, and a different reading would change the included set (Supplementary Files S8 and S7).
- **Counts are of reports.** The 1,080 included items are reports: they describe 1,078 distinct studies on the definite same-data links (1,076 counting the partial overlaps), the five RoB 2 report rows are four trials, and S526 and S539 (two US utility studies) are flagged for verification of sample overlap before any pooling.
- **Search scope.** SSRN and Westlaw/Lexis were not searched (Supplementary File S37, Known limitations).

*Source of every figure: Supplementary File S38; checked by Supplementary File S2.*

### 5.1 Confidence in the evidence and in the review's own process

This is the author's tentative characterisation, not a GRADE judgment (none has been made): the direction-of-association statements rest on a small quantitative subset (18, 6 and 20 studies), AI-extracted and AI-coded values, mostly observational designs, and a screening stage that closed early. Reasons follow.

### 5.2 Incomplete screening

- Full-text retrieval closed at 62.2%: 1,382 of 3,659 records (1,200 not retrievable, 182 wrong file delivered) were never assessed, and the unassessed set is systematically older (Section 3.7). Some may be duplicates of included studies, some not eligible; the direction of any bias is unknown.
- 151 of the 1,197 full-text excludes are E10 (inaccessible text), i.e. never judged on content.
- The full-text decisions were made by the AI, and 445 of the 2,277 (19.5%) carry human verification or adjudication: the author confirmed 197 includes (S001–S200) and adjudicated 248 further decisions, which makes 281 human-checked includes and 164 human-checked excludes; the other 1,832 AI decisions have not been checked by a human. 73 decided rows had no reviewer label recorded at the time (an AI label was inferred for them on 2026-10-14 from the exclusion log and extraction records), and the author has since ruled on all of them. A prioritised queue for further human review exists (Supplementary File S39).
- **A blind AI re-read of the exclusions was adjudicated by the author.** Two non-Claude models (Codex, Gemini), blind to the original decision, re-read 70 full-text exclusions whose PDF was available: 62 drawn at random, stratified by exclusion code, and 8 from the priority queue. The models disagreed with the AI on 52 of them (49 from the random sample and 3 from outside it), most often under wrong topic and engineering only. The author adjudicated all 52: 49 exclusions were confirmed and 3 were reversed to inclusions (S1165, S1166, S1167). Among the 62 randomly sampled exclusions, 3 were therefore false exclusions (4.8%, 95% Wilson interval 1.7–13.3%); scaled to the 1,038 content-based exclusions (all codes except the 151 inaccessible texts and the 8 duplicates, which were never sampled), that is about 50 studies (interval 17–138; about 37 when scaled within exclusion code). This replaces the earlier arithmetic of roughly 532 further includes, which took the models' verdicts at face value. The estimate has three limits: it counts only the exclusions the models flagged, it was made under the narrow reading of eligibility criterion 2 that the author adopted after seeing the disagreement, and it rests on a sample of 62. In the other direction the models questioned 4 inclusions, and the author excluded all 4; the consistency passes that followed under the same narrow reading moved 80 further included reports to the exclusions (84 in all). The author's reading list is Supplementary File S40.
- At title/abstract stage the human second pass covered the 3,665 include-plus-unsure records only; the 22,557 records the AI excluded were not human-checked, so any wrongly excluded record is invisible to this review. The 99.8% agreement on the reviewed set is unusually high and was flagged by the project itself (Supplementary Files S37 and S17).

### 5.3 Missing or thin data

- 10 studies were extracted from abstract or metadata only (0.9%); none has an effect-size row, so the SWiM syntheses are unaffected by them, but descriptive counts and ratings include them. **That count is a floor:** it counts one note prefix, and a sparse-record audit (Supplementary File S41) found 53 more rows with at least one sign of shallow extraction (none has notes saying that only the abstract or citation was read, and 15 have a recorded location that cites the abstract alone; a verification queue, not proof), of which 1 carries an effect-size row. There is no second extractor: extracted values have not been checked against the source papers.
- 51 of 130 JBI ratings are "High concern", which here means sparse extraction rather than a poor study. For CASP and MMAT, several items are "Can't tell" for every study (for example CASP item 3, research design justified) because extraction did not capture methodological reporting. Ratings are rule-based and unreviewed by a human.
- Of 60 effect-size rows, 9 give a confidence interval and 26 a standard error; estimates are as the papers report them and none is pooled.

## 6. Conclusion

The evidence assembled here shows that legal and administrative conditions are studied in association with water and sanitation access across a wide range of settings, most often descriptively and rarely with designs able to support causal claims (63 of 1,080 studies). Where quantitative estimates exist they are too heterogeneous to pool, and their direction is most consistent for legal-status, tenure and recognition mechanisms and for assistance and participation mechanisms, least consistent for ownership, regulation and administrative-structure mechanisms. These statements are provisional: extraction and appraisal were AI-conducted and only partly verified, screening of the full-text pool is incomplete, and the protocol was not registered in advance. The review does not establish the *legal last mile* as a general mechanism; it describes where evidence exists, where it is thin, and what a human-verified update would need to check first. ### 6.1 Implications for research and practice

*Conditional on human verification.* (1) Policy readers should not take this review as evidence that any single legal or administrative reform causes better access; the designs that could show that are few. (2) The next research step the repository supports is verification, not expansion: complete the human second pass of screening and a second extraction, fetch the full texts of the shallow extractions and the unassessed older records, and finish the check of overlap between the AMSTAR 2 reviews and the primary studies. (3) If the comparative frame of the underlying doctoral project (Netherlands, Canada, Brazil) is to be tested, targeted searches for Dutch and Canadian evidence are needed, because the corpus holds very few single-country studies for the first two (Section 3.7).
