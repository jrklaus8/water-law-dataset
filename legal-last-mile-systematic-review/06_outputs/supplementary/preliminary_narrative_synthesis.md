# Preliminary Narrative Synthesis: What Can Be Inferred So Far

**Status: a preliminary qualitative/narrative synthesis, not a validated
systematic-review conclusion.** This draws substantive inferences from the
509 studies extracted under Phase 8 — what recurs across them, where they
agree, and where they don't — rather than merely counting them (see
`preliminary_corpus_characterization.md` for the counts). Per
`PROJECT_SPEC.md` §15, a qualitative mechanism synthesis with no pooled
estimate ("Option D") is a fully legitimate outcome for this review, not a
fallback from a "real" result. Per §14, none of what follows may be read as
establishing the Legal Last Mile hypothesis, or as a risk-of-bias-adjusted
or effect-size-weighted conclusion: Phase 9 (risk of bias) is rated for only
2% of studies, Phase 11 (quantitative feasibility) has cleared no family, and
28% of the eligible pool has been full-text screened. Citations below are
illustrative, not exhaustive — representative of a pattern found repeatedly
across the corpus, not a complete list of every study showing it.

## 1. The pattern that recurs most consistently: legal/administrative status gates access independently of physical infrastructure

Across dozens of studies spanning Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa, South
Asia, and the United States, the same structural story repeats: households
are excluded from formal water or sanitation service **not because the
network cannot reach them, but because their legal or administrative status
disqualifies them from it.**

- **Jurisdictional/tenure status:** Tooleville, California and Bommanahalli,
  Bangalore are both excluded from their adjacent municipal water networks
  by unincorporated/peri-urban jurisdictional status, not physical distance
  (S412, Ranganathan & Balazs 2015). Delhi's Kathputli Colony's water access
  depends on Slum Areas Act 1956 notification status, leaving it "an
  exceptional grant rather than an enforceable right" despite a 2002 Supreme
  Court Article 21 ruling (S416, Roy 2013) — a pattern echoed directly in
  S502 (Chidambaram 2020), where notified-vs-non-notified slum
  classification in Delhi determines formal piped-connection entitlement.
- **Formal recognition of community providers:** In Veracruz, Mexico,
  community water organizations have no formal legal recognition at all —
  unlike in Chile, Ecuador, or their Central American peers — with a
  "municipal exclusivity" legal argument used to deny it (S508). In
  La Paz/El Alto, Bolivia, self-organized ASD water cooperatives are legally
  required to register with the Ministry for Water but rarely do, "due to
  the complicated and costly bureaucratic steps required," while the formal
  utility EPSAS is itself legally barred from serving "non-constructible"
  hazard zones — formally excluding entire hillside neighborhoods from the
  regulated network (S511).
- **Caste and social status:** In peri-urban Kathmandu, a registered
  water-user committee excludes Dalit households despite their prior-use
  rights and a national mandatory-representation policy (S498, Shrestha,
  Joshi & Roth 2020).
- **Racial and settlement history:** A North Carolina community was excluded
  from municipal service for decades by racial municipal underbounding,
  only annexed and connected in 2020 (S418, Brown et al. 2023, citing the
  Irongate Drive case).

This is the single most repeated substantive finding across the corpus, and
it recurs across enough different legal systems, income levels, and
continents that it looks less like a jurisdiction-specific quirk and more
like a structural feature of how formal water/sanitation systems are
administered. **This is consistent with — and lends real, if
methodologically modest, support to — the Legal Last Mile thesis that
administrative/legal status operates as a distinct barrier layer, separate
from and additional to physical infrastructure availability.** It does not
establish the thesis: as noted in §6 below, the studies behind this pattern
are overwhelmingly qualitative and observational.

## 2. Institutional fragmentation is the most pervasive single theme in the literature

263 of 509 studies (52%) code positive for institutional fragmentation — by
far the most common mechanism sub-code in the corpus. The pattern is
remarkably consistent in its shape: overlapping or unclear mandates between
national ministries, municipal governments, utilities, and donor/NGO actors
produce coordination gaps that translate directly into unequal access,
independent of any single actor's intent.

- Malawi's rural water supply is split between the Ministry of Water and
  Irrigation Development and locally delegated bodies (district assemblies,
  contractors, water point committees, or NGOs), while urban/peri-urban
  supply runs through parastatal water boards under a separate legal
  instrument — producing a documented urban-rural access gap the national
  water policy's equity language does nothing to close because it sets no
  differentiated implementation targets (S510, Adams & Smiley 2018).
- Uganda's water/sanitation mandate is split across three ministries, a
  fragmentation directly linked to a 65%-versus-40% safely-managed-access
  gap between two towns (S402, Sakaya et al. 2025).
- Nigerian water-point provision spans federal, state, local, NGO, and donor
  actors simultaneously, with 45.8% of 606 surveyed facilities
  non-functional (S495, Samuel, Agbola & Olojede 2021).
- Kigali's fecal sludge management sits across 23 separate government
  laws/strategies/regulations with overlapping mandates (S475, Murebwayire
  et al. 2025).

Fragmentation is coded alongside a wide range of outcome types (connection,
functionality, coverage, quality) and does not point to one single
mechanism of harm — sometimes it manifests as unclear responsibility for
maintenance, sometimes as duplicated or contradictory eligibility criteria,
sometimes as simple non-communication between levels of government. That
diversity of manifestation is itself informative: fragmentation looks like a
genuinely general phenomenon in water/sanitation governance rather than a
single specific bureaucratic pathology.

## 3. Administrative discretion cuts both ways — it is simultaneously the mechanism of accommodation and of arbitrary exclusion

`discretion_accommodation` is coded TRUE for 61% of the corpus, the most
prevalent of the four core mechanisms — but the studies behind that number
describe two genuinely opposite phenomena, and the review's own coding
framework (`CODEBOOK.md`) treats them as the same family only because both
involve discretionary administrative judgment, not because they have the
same normative valence:

- **Discretion as accommodation:** Free Basic Water indigent-registration
  schemes (Cape Town, S492/S504), hardship-based eligibility criteria for
  latrine-construction support (Nigeria's SHAWN programme, S509), and
  "street-level discretion" among US utility staff — bending rules,
  expediting assistance-program enrollment, waiving fees (S415, Ward et al.
  2026) — are all cases where discretion is the *route to* access for
  people who would otherwise fail a rigid formal criterion.
- **Discretion as arbitrary exclusion:** The same Cape Town case shows
  Water Management Devices installed "without informed consent from some
  residents" and legalized only by a Constitutional Court ruling (Mazibuko)
  that itself became controversial; political interference skews
  water-point siting in Nigeria (S509) and produces "favouritism during
  relief support" as the single most common grievance in Karonga, Malawi's
  disaster-relief distribution (36% of complaints); weak by-law enforcement
  against illegal connections in South African municipalities is explicitly
  tied to political complicity (S484, Monyai et al. 2022).

The honest reading is that discretion is not inherently good or bad for
access — it is a **release valve that can go either way** depending on who
holds it and what constrains its use. A rigid, non-discretionary eligibility
rule reliably excludes anyone outside its terms; a discretionary one can
either rescue such cases (accommodation) or introduce new, unaccountable
grounds for exclusion (favoritism, corruption, political interference). This
is a genuinely interesting and, on the current evidence, under-theorized
tension for the dissertation's Administrative Ghost/administrative-capital
framing to engage with directly.

## 4. Economic and procedural burden compounds legal exclusion, and often falls hardest on those already excluded by eligibility rules

Fees, tariffs, and procedural steps are coded in 167, 167, and 43 studies
respectively, and they interact with the eligibility/status mechanisms in
§1 rather than operating independently:

- In Darjeeling, formal water connection requires three separate legal
  land/residency documents plus a tiered connection fee of USD 250–520
  (S497, Shah & Badiger 2020) — documentation and cost operating as a
  combined, compounding barrier.
- In Nairobi's Mukuru informal settlement, a documented "poverty penalty" of
  172% higher per-m³ water cost, combined with a KES 5,000 connection fee,
  was severe enough that a dedicated micro-loan facility had to be built
  around it (S477).
- In rural Malawi, a private household well costs roughly MWK 600,000
  against a local minimum wage of MWK 18,000/month — a burden an order of
  magnitude beyond the ~US$56 urban connection fee available to
  differently-classified households (S510).
- In La Paz/El Alto, cooperative tariffs (BOB 5–10/month) sit well below the
  formal utility's regulated rate structure, but come with a separate
  BOB 500 connection fee and no access to the formal tariff-equalization
  scheme cooperatives are excluded from by registration status (S511).

The consistent shape here is that **economic burden is rarely the sole
mechanism** — it typically operates as a second gate behind an eligibility
or documentation requirement, meaning removing the fee alone would not by
itself resolve access for households who fail the underlying legal-status
test.

## 5. Enforcement operates in two directions, against two different targets — and this matters for policy interpretation

The `enforcement` mechanism (coded in 40% of the corpus, sanction/
disconnection sub-codes specifically in 41 and 35 studies) is not a single
phenomenon. Reading across studies, it splits cleanly into:

- **Enforcement against households/users:** disconnection for non-payment
  (30% of Blantyre's piped connections disconnected within 5 years, S500),
  septic-system enforcement via fines and arrests in Lowndes County, Alabama
  since 2002 (S478), landlords fined by police for unauthorized road-cutting
  in Dhaka (S469).
- **Enforcement against duty-bearers (states, utilities, regulators):**
  France's LEMA (2006) statutory disconnection prohibition, later reinforced
  by administrative tribunals annulling municipal anti-disconnection
  arrêtés for exceeding municipal competence (S400); a 2023 US DOJ/HHS
  interim agreement compelling Lowndes County to provide sanitation access
  "regardless of ability to pay" (S478, the same case appearing on both
  sides of this split); a 2021 Brazilian regulatory intervention (Arsae-MG)
  associated with a ~19-percentage-point increase in social-tariff
  enrollment (S470); Uganda's presidential directive suspending water
  disconnections nationwide during COVID-19 (S491).

The same mechanism label therefore captures both the state disciplining
poor households and the state (or a court, or a regulator) disciplining
under-performing duty-bearers on behalf of poor households. **Enforcement's
net effect on access depends entirely on which direction is dominant in a
given legal system at a given time** — a distinction the review's current
boolean coding does not itself preserve, and one worth flagging as a coding
refinement for any future extraction pass or for the dissertation's
doctrinal chapters to develop further.

## 6. Where the evidence is closest to causal, it is thin, mixed in direction, and cautions against a simple story

Only 30 of 509 studies (6%) use an experimental or quasi-experimental design,
and only 25 have a locatable, non-fabricated effect estimate at all
(`effect_sizes.csv`). Reading across just this subset:

- Several point in the direction the Legal Last Mile framework would
  predict: WRUA legal-membership status reduced a water-poverty index by
  14–32% across three independent quasi-experimental estimators (S404);
  Tribal regulatory oversight was associated with lower groundwater-decline
  risk (OR = 0.62, p < 0.001, S358); Recife's ZEIS zoning-law designation was
  associated with a ~23-percentage-point rise in sewage-network access
  14–17 years later (S398, a genuine difference-in-differences design).
- But the clearest causal-design study in the entire corpus (S085, Gaikwad
  & Thomas, a genuine cluster-randomized field experiment) complicates a
  simple "add legal recognition, get access" story directly: bureaucratic
  assistance alone and political coordination alone **each had no
  significant average effect** — the 19-percentage-point formalization gain
  only appeared when both were present together. Mechanisms in this
  literature look conjunctive/interactive more often than additive.
- Genuine null and attenuated findings are also present and should be
  weighed, not filtered out (`PROJECT_SPEC.md` §11): S037 found no
  statistically significant difference in poor drinking-water exposure
  between disadvantaged and comparison communities; a cluster-RCT of a
  WASH-institution intervention found no effect on child diarrhea or growth
  despite a measurable institutional improvement (S294); a US governance-
  structure effect on tariffs (mayor- vs. manager-led systems) attenuates
  from significant to non-significant once fiscal and community covariates
  are added (S471).

**The honest summary of this subsection is that the small quantitative
evidence base is directionally consistent with the broader qualitative
pattern in §1, but is far too thin, heterogeneous in design, and
unweighted by risk of bias to support any claim about the *size* of the
effect, and it already contains at least one important disconfirmation of
the simplest version of the causal story** (mechanisms interact rather than
add).

## 7. Where inference should stop: geographic and jurisdictional limits

The corpus is heavily concentrated in Brazil (63 studies), India (40), South
Africa (37), the United States (31), and Kenya (29). **Canada appears in only
7 studies, and the Netherlands does not appear among the top 15 countries at
all.** Two of the dissertation's three comparator jurisdictions are
therefore essentially unrepresented in this empirical-evidence pipeline.
Everything above should be read as a pattern found predominantly in
Global-South and U.S. contexts, often in urban-informal or rural settings
with weak formal tenure — **not yet as evidence about how these mechanisms
operate in the Netherlands's or Ontario's more fully formalized
administrative-law contexts**, which the dissertation's separate doctrinal
and judicial-dataset strands, not this empirical review, are positioned to
address (`PROJECT_SPEC.md` §9–10).

## 8. Bottom line

**What the evidence to date is consistent with:** legal and administrative
status — recognition, documentation, registration, jurisdictional
boundaries, notification/tenure classification — recurs across an unusually
wide range of independent studies, countries, and legal systems as a
distinguishable barrier to formal water/sanitation access, operating
alongside and sometimes instead of physical infrastructure constraints.
Institutional fragmentation and administrative discretion are the most
pervasive specific forms this takes. This is a real, non-trivial pattern in
the literature and a defensible qualitative-synthesis finding in its own
right (`PROJECT_SPEC.md` §15 Option D).

**What it does not yet establish:** the causal size of this effect (too few
causal-design studies, none risk-of-bias rated, none pooled); whether the
mechanisms interact additively or conjunctively in general (the one clearest
experimental study suggests conjunctively, contradicting the simplest
version of the story); whether the pattern holds in the Netherlands/Ontario
administrative-law contexts the dissertation ultimately compares (near-absent
from this corpus); and whether the pattern would survive a completed search
(SSRN and Westlaw/Lexis were never searched) and a completed risk-of-bias
pass (98% of studies remain unrated).

This synthesis should be revisited, not treated as final, once Phase 9
ratings are substantially more complete and Phase 11 has made an actual
per-family feasibility determination.
