### 2A.5 Gaps visible in the records

- **Geographic:** Empirical research on the legal last mile is heavily concentrated in middle-income urbanising jurisdictions (India 121, Brazil 80, South Africa 77, Ghana 51) and the United States (62), where last-mile exclusion is studied primarily at the informal-settlement tenure, municipal incorporation and utility-connection boundary: in the four middle-income countries, 187 of 329 studies (57%) code a formal-connection outcome and 144 (44%) an eligibility mechanism. By contrast, high-coverage welfare states with near-universal physical networks are sparsely represented (for example, 15 single-country studies in Canada, 12 of them concerning the drinking water of First Nations and other Indigenous communities (advisories, infrastructure, regulation and jurisdictional gaps), and 1 in the Netherlands), leaving administrative exclusion mechanisms within universal-network systems, such as tariff hardship exemptions, tenant billing rules and administrative redress, comparatively under-examined in the evidence this review identified. 552 (51%) of studies sit in the ten commonest single countries.
- **Design:** 5 randomised trials (4 distinct) and 53 quasi-experimental studies against 426 (40%) studies appraised with the project's own non-validated framework (the residual category for designs no published instrument fits, including doctrinal and documentary studies).
- **Mechanisms/outcomes:** redress and review mechanisms and application-process outcomes are thinly coded (see 2A.2); sanitation access less than water.
- **Retrieval:** 1,382 title/abstract includes were never assessed. Their median publication year is 2014 versus 2018 for assessed records; 8.9% were published before 2000 versus 3.0%, and 30.8% in 2020 or later versus 42.7% — the unassessed set is older, so the corpus under-represents earlier literature.

## 2B. Tentative interpretations (not findings — each rests on the numbers cited and could be wrong)

1. **The literature documents co-occurrence far more than causation.** With 58 (5%) causal-capable designs (the mechanism-certainty code now follows the design, §2A.1), the base can say that legal/administrative features and access outcomes are associated across many settings, but rarely that one causes the other. *Rests on:* §2A.1 counts. *Would change if:* the 1,382 unassessed records or future searches contain many more designs with credible identification.
2. **Recognition and formal status tend to go with better access (Family A), but the pattern is thin.** 10 of 13 Favourable, 1 Unfavourable (S104 tenure and interruptions): S104 appraised with JBI Cross-Sectional. *Rests on:* the SWiM coding, a judgment by the same AI. Not a pooled estimate and not certainty-graded.
3. **Assistance and trust mechanisms look uniformly favourable (Family B) — but on three studies with three different mechanisms.** The consistency may reflect small numbers or reporting of positive findings; nothing here excludes either.
4. **Ownership and price: private ownership goes with worse affordability in the three studies (S526, S539, S749), but the two US samples probably overlap**, so this may be two independent samples, not three (`linked_reports_2026-09-28.csv` LR09, an audit inference).
5. **Most institutional analysis in this corpus is descriptive-qualitative and concentrated on fragmentation, discretion and fees.** This may reflect what the search and the closed retrieval surfaced (English-language, post-2010, database-indexed) as much as what the world contains.
6. **The evidence is concentrated where networks are incomplete and thin where they are near-universal.** Middle-income urbanising countries and the United States dominate, and high-coverage welfare states are represented by few studies (15 single-country studies in Canada, mostly on First Nations drinking water, and 1 in the Netherlands), so how administrative exclusion operates inside universal-network systems (tariff hardship exemptions, tenant billing rules, administrative redress) cannot be assessed from this evidence base.

## 3. Confidence and limitations

### 3.1 Overall confidence: low to very low for any causal or comparative claim

This is the author's tentative characterisation, not a GRADE judgment (none has been made): the direction-of-association statements rest on a small quantitative subset (13, 3 and 15 studies), AI-extracted and AI-coded values, mostly observational designs, and a screening stage that closed early. Reasons follow.

### 3.2 Incomplete screening

- Full-text retrieval closed at 62.2%: 1,382 of 3,659 records (1,200 not retrievable, 182 wrong file delivered) were never assessed, and the unassessed set is systematically older (§2A.5). Some may be duplicates of included studies, some not eligible; the direction of any bias is unknown.
- 151 of the 1,205 full-text excludes are E10 (inaccessible text), i.e. never judged on content.
- The full-text decisions were made by the AI, and 459 of the 2,277 (20.2%) carry human verification or adjudication: the author confirmed 197 includes (S001–S200) and adjudicated 262 further decisions, which makes 287 human-checked includes and 172 human-checked excludes; the other 1,818 AI decisions have not been checked by a human. 73 decided rows had no reviewer label recorded at the time (an AI label was inferred for them on 2026-10-14 from the exclusion log and extraction records, open item A2), and the author has since ruled on all of them. A prioritised queue for further human review exists (`02_screening/full_text/REVIEWER_2_PRIORITY_QUEUE_README.md`).
- **A blind AI re-read of the exclusions was adjudicated by the author.** Two non-Claude models (Codex, Gemini), blind to the original decision, re-read 70 full-text exclusions whose PDF was available: 62 drawn at random, stratified by exclusion code, and 8 from the priority queue. The models disagreed with the AI on 52 of them (49 from the random sample and 3 from outside it), most often under wrong topic and engineering only. The author adjudicated all 52: 49 exclusions were confirmed and 3 were reversed to inclusions (S1165, S1166, S1167). Among the 62 randomly sampled exclusions, 3 were therefore false exclusions (4.8%, 95% Wilson interval 1.7–13.3%); scaled to the 1,046 content-based exclusions (all codes except the 151 inaccessible texts and the 8 duplicates, which were never sampled), that is about 51 studies (interval 17–139; about 37 when scaled within exclusion code). This replaces the earlier arithmetic of roughly 539 further includes, which took the models' verdicts at face value. The estimate has three limits: it counts only the exclusions the models flagged, it was made under the narrow reading of eligibility criterion 2 that the author adopted after seeing the disagreement, and it rests on a sample of 62. In the other direction the models questioned 4 inclusions, and the author excluded all 4; the consistency passes that followed under the same narrow reading moved 88 further included reports to the exclusions (92 in all). The author's reading list is `02_screening/full_text/A16_ADJUDICATION_SHEET_2026-10-04.md` (open item A16).
- At title/abstract stage the human second pass covered the 3,665 include-plus-unsure records only; the 22,557 records the AI excluded were not human-checked, so any wrongly excluded record is invisible to this review. The 99.8% agreement on the reviewed set is unusually high and was flagged by the project itself (`README.md`; `AI_USE_STATEMENT.md`).

### 3.3 Missing or thin data

- 10 studies were extracted from abstract or metadata only (0.9%); none has an effect-size row, so the SWiM syntheses are unaffected by them, but descriptive counts and ratings include them. **That count is a floor:** it counts one note prefix, and a sparse-record audit (`05_analysis/descriptive/SPARSE_RECORD_AUDIT_2026-10-04.md`) found 53 more rows with at least one sign of shallow extraction (none has notes saying that only the abstract or citation was read, and 15 have a recorded location that cites the abstract alone; a verification queue, not proof), of which 1 carries an effect-size row. There is no second extractor: extracted values have not been checked against the source papers.
- 51 of 127 JBI ratings are "High concern", which here means sparse extraction rather than a poor study. For CASP and MMAT, several items are "Can't tell" for every study (for example CASP item 3, research design justified) because extraction did not capture methodological reporting. Ratings are rule-based and unreviewed by a human.
- Of 52 effect-size rows, 8 give a confidence interval and 21 a standard error; estimates are as the papers report them and none is pooled.

### 3.4 Unresolved classifications — the two you asked about

**Are the AMSTAR 2 studies actually systematic reviews? Partly confirmed, partly not.**

- 18 studies carry AMSTAR 2. Eleven further studies had a secondary-review design class but were reclassified to `NONE` in 2026-09-28/29 after an eligibility check found them to be self-described narrative, conceptual or documentary reviews that never claim a systematic search (`RISK_OF_BIAS.md` §4). On 2026-10-14 the PI ruled on all of them (open item A25): ten were excluded (E05, no systematic method or primary data, or E03, outside the narrow scope) and one, S480, stayed included under the Legal Institutional Evidence Appraisal Framework. No included study is now tagged `NONE`, so the gap of a validated instrument for a non-systematic review no longer affects an included study, though it would again if such reviews were admitted.
- Of the 18 that kept AMSTAR 2, each was classed as a secondary systematic review. The recorded fields (publication type, design, method text, sample) support that unevenly: 12 name a registration, PRISMA or JBI method, 14 state a count of included studies, and 1 (S521) shows at most one weak signal (the word "systematic" or a review type) — a way to order full-text checks, not a finding (`05_analysis/descriptive/DATA_QUALITY_AUDIT_2026-09-29.md` §3). None of them is now extracted from abstract or metadata only. S319, S325 and S344 were read in full text on 2026-10-02 (S319 searched one database; S325 and S344 are single-author reviews with weak methods, but all 3 state a search, a selection process and a synthesis, so all keep AMSTAR 2); S418, S438 and S052 were read in full text on 2026-10-03 (S418 is described as a narrative/scoping review with a documented search, a hybrid kept eligible with a noted caveat; all 3 state a search, selection and synthesis, so all keep AMSTAR 2). Five reviews that earlier drafts of this bullet discussed (S324, S326, S327, S329 and S697) are no longer in the corpus: they were excluded in the consistency passes of 2026-10-13 and 2026-10-14, S326 because it states no search or selection method and the other four under the narrow reading of criterion 2.
- **All 18 systematic reviews received a formal AMSTAR 2 confidence rating** (17 Critically Low and 1 Low, S537), primarily reflecting absent pre-registered protocols, single-database searches, unappraised primary studies or missing lists of excluded studies. **So AMSTAR 2 so far tells the reader little beyond "low confidence".**
- *Tentative flag (this report's own observation, not a project ruling):* AMSTAR 2 was designed for reviews of healthcare interventions that include randomised or non-randomised studies; several of these reviews are realist, scoping or mapping reviews of qualitative and policy literature, for which a rating would be out-of-design even with full information. A decision on whether to keep AMSTAR 2 for these, or use a different instrument, is open.
- Reviews are secondary evidence and are never pooled as if primary. Whether their primary studies are also separate rows in this corpus was checked on 2026-10-04 for only 7 of the 18 reviews (those whose PDFs were to hand): their reference lists cite 48 distinct corpus studies, S319 (21) and S325 (19) cite cite ten or more. This is an upper bound (background citations count) and does not read the included-studies tables, so the real double-counting risk is **still unresolved**, and 11 reviews are unchecked (`05_analysis/descriptive/AMSTAR2_OVERLAP_CHECK_2026-10-04.md`).

**Do the randomised trials need the cluster version of RoB 2? Yes for all five — one unit is inferred, and S366 has now been re-read in full.**

- All 5 RoB 2 studies (S057, S085, S294, S366, S879) were checked and are **cluster-randomised**, not individually randomised, and were appraised with the cluster-trial variant (`04_quality/appraisal_forms/`). Four state cluster randomisation in their own extracted design field.
- **S879** (Kenya) records only "randomized controlled trial"; cluster randomisation at compound level is *inferred* from the recorded unit of intervention and flagged "verify against source paper before finalizing rating" in its own tool field. Its rating is provisional.
- **S366** was re-extracted and re-appraised on 2026-09-29 from the full-text PDF you supplied (its earlier abstract-only, low-confidence rating is superseded): Domains 1 and 3 Low, Domains 2 and 5 Some concerns, Domain 4 Some concerns for the primary outcomes and High for the self-reported satisfaction/behaviour indices; Figure 1 and the supplement were not read. S366 and S294 report the same DRC cluster trial, so the 5 RoB 2 studies are **4 distinct trials** (but different surveys: 1,312 households in S366, 3,283 in S294). All five rate "Some concerns" overall; S057, S085, S294 and S879 still use many "No information" answers because extraction did not capture trial-conduct details.
- A keyword scan of design fields in the other 1,067 studies found no further randomised design under another tool. **S189** is a process evaluation conducted "in connection with a randomised controlled trial" (Orissa, India); it was appraised with ROBINS-I as a quasi-experimental study, and its full appraisal form covers the confounding domain only. Whether the parent trial itself is in the corpus, and whether any of its outcomes belong under RoB 2 cluster, is worth confirming. (The scan covered design and measure fields only, not full texts.)
