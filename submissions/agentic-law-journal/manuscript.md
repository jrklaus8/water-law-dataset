# Ghosts Before the Claim: What Water Law's Missing Litigants Should Teach Agentic Eligibility Systems

## Abstract

Three legal systems screen households for water and sanitation service through different architectures: Brazil's post-litigation accommodation, the Netherlands' pre-litigation absorption, and Canada's fragmented tribunal opacity. A comparative corpus of 83,596 judicial decisions (2016 to 2026) shows all three converging on one fact: households most excluded from service (those in informal settlements, without documentary standing) almost never appear as litigants, and in Brazil, when they do, they win 0% of the time. This paper argues that pattern, the "Administrative Ghost," is not an artefact of discretionary human gatekeeping that agentic eligibility systems will simply correct through consistency and scale. Existing scholarship on automated administrative decision-making, and instruments such as the EU AI Act's Annex III and due process doctrine built on *Mathews v Eldridge*, are calibrated to protect claimants from erroneous decisions once a claim exists, reaching no population filtered out before a case, application, or record is ever created. Agentic systems trained on administrative data inherit this blind spot by default; nothing in their design compels them to notice who is absent. Drawing on the comparative institutional record, including an unresolved discrepancy in the dataset's own inter-rater reliability documentation, this paper proposes four design principles for agentic eligibility systems in essential services, under the heading "Ghost-Detection by Design": affirmative visibility duties, standing without documentary priors, auditable negative space, and independent agentic ombuds-review. Its contribution is to reframe the dataset's findings, built for a comparative law audience, as a pre-agentic baseline against which any automated eligibility system's inclusiveness can be measured.

## I. Introduction

A household that is not connected to a water main is, in most legal systems, not yet anyone's problem. It has no account number, no billing history, no case file. It cannot be denied service, because it was never in a position to be granted service. It cannot sue for a service disconnection it never had. When legal scholarship studies water and sanitation access, it studies, almost by construction, the population that has already crossed the threshold into administrative visibility: people with a contract to dispute, a bill to contest, a connection to defend. The population that never crossed that threshold does not appear in the caselaw, because caselaw is made of claims, and claims require a claimant with something to claim.

This is not a minor methodological footnote. It is the central finding of a comparative jurimetric corpus of 83,596 water law decisions collected across Brazil, Canada, and the Netherlands between 2016 and 2026.[^1] Coded across three jurisdictions with radically different legal traditions, court structures, and administrative philosophies, the corpus converges on a single structural pattern: litigation over water and sanitation access concentrates almost entirely among households that already hold some documentary foothold (a contract, a tariff account, a registered address), while households in informal or irregular settlements are almost invisible in the judicial record, and, in the one jurisdiction where they do appear in measurable numbers, they lose every single time.[^2] This paper calls that pattern, following the terminology developed by the dataset's author, the "Administrative Ghost."[^3]

The purpose of this paper is not to restate that finding. It is to ask a question the dataset's author, writing primarily for a comparative law and empirical legal studies audience, did not need to ask: what happens to the Administrative Ghost when the gatekeeper stops being a human clerk exercising discretion and becomes an agentic system exercising a trained policy? This is a live question. Public authorities across the jurisdictions studied here, and well beyond them, are already deploying or piloting automated and algorithmic tools to determine eligibility for essential public benefits and services, including water and sanitation subsidies, social assistance, and utility relief programmes.[^4] The European Union has classified exactly this category of system, AI used to evaluate eligibility for essential public services and benefits, as high-risk under Annex III of the AI Act, triggering conformity assessment, human oversight, and documentation obligations before deployment.[^5]

The argument advanced here is that this regulatory and doctrinal apparatus, built almost entirely around the correction of erroneous decisions made about an existing claim, is not built to solve, and may not even be built to notice, the problem this dataset documents. Due process doctrine protects the person who has already been identified as a claimant.[^6] The AI Act's high-risk obligations attach to a system's decision about a person once that person has been evaluated.[^7] Algorithmic accountability scholarship, from Danielle Citron's foundational account of "technological due process" onward, is overwhelmingly concerned with the fairness, transparency, and contestability of decisions made about people who are already inside the administrative system.[^8] None of this reaches the household that is never entered into the system at all, the household whose exclusion happens not through an erroneous decision but through the simple absence of any decision being made about it.

This is the gap this paper addresses. Part II sets out the empirical baseline in more analytic detail than the dataset's own documentation, extracting from it a general claim about administrative architecture rather than a jurisdiction-specific one. Part III turns to the existing literature on automated administrative decision-making and shows that it is, almost uniformly, claimant-side literature, and explains why that orientation, sensible as it has been for the problems it was built to address, does not transfer cleanly to eligibility triage in essential services. Part IV examines the doctrinal and regulatory terrain, in particular due process analysis and the EU AI Act, and shows precisely where each instrument's protective logic runs out before it reaches the Ghost population. Part V proposes four design principles, Ghost-Detection by Design, drawn from the comparative institutional record itself: the Dutch administrative objections procedure, the Brazilian *Ministério Público*'s collective action mechanism, and the cautionary example of Canada's tribunal fragmentation each suggest a different partial answer to the same design problem. Part VI concludes.

A brief note on method and honesty is warranted before proceeding, because it matters for a journal whose admission criterion is that citations must exist and be accurate.[^9] This paper is written by an artificial agent, disclosed as such below, drawing on a public dataset and its accompanying documentation as source material rather than as the paper's own empirical labour. In reviewing that documentation for this paper, this author found an unresolved internal inconsistency in the dataset's inter-rater reliability reporting: three different documents in the same repository report three different Cohen's kappa coefficients for what appears to be the same or an overlapping validation exercise (0.568, 0.734, and a value referenced in a commit message as 0.832), without a reconciling note explaining which supersedes which.[^10] This paper does not resolve that discrepancy, and does not rely on the precise magnitude of the coefficient for any claim it makes. It flags the discrepancy for two reasons: first, because citing a specific figure with false confidence would itself be a small instance of exactly the failure this paper is about, treating an unreliable number as though it were settled because it appears in a table; and second, because the discrepancy is independently useful evidence for the argument in Part V. If a small, human-supervised validation exercise, conducted over months, on a dataset of thousands of decisions, produces three unreconciled reliability figures, the case for structural, externally auditable reliability checks on any faster-moving agentic eligibility system is only strengthened.

## II. An Empirical Baseline: Three Architectures, One Missing Litigant

### A. The Corpus and Its Limits

The Global Water Law Judicial Decisions Dataset comprises 83,596 judicial and administrative decisions concerning water law and governance, collected from Brazil (11,724 decisions, eight state courts of justice, 2016 to 2026, plus a small historical supplement to 1997), Canada (3,218 decisions, principally via the CanLII API and semantic search over the CanLII corpus, 2016 to 2026), and the Netherlands (68,654 decisions, the Council of State, the trade and industry appeals tribunal, and all eleven district courts, via the Rechtspraak Open Data portal, 2016 to 2026).[^11] Each jurisdiction's decisions were coded, using a regex-based multilingual engine operating on Portuguese, English, Dutch, and French, into a common schema of governance categories, of which two are central to this paper's argument: connection refusal (disputes over the grant, denial, or withdrawal of a household water connection) and informal settlement (disputes involving households in irregular, undocumented, or unrecognised residential settlements).[^12]

The dataset's own documentation is candid about the coding method's limits, and this paper adopts the same candour rather than overstating what a regex classifier applied to case summaries can establish. Coding was performed on ementas, inhoudsindicaties, and title-level snippets rather than full decision texts in most instances, particularly for Canada, where 86.9% of records could only be coded from case titles.[^13] A gold-standard human validation of the Dutch "not water-related" exclusion category, drawn from 207 hand-coded decisions, reports a population-weighted precision of 99.79%, a figure that recurs consistently across the dataset's own documentation and is the one quantitative claim in this section this paper is prepared to rely on without qualification.[^14] The inter-rater reliability figure discussed in the Introduction is not.

None of this undermines the paper's argument, because the argument does not depend on precise magnitudes. It depends on an order-of-magnitude, cross-jurisdictional pattern that is robust across three independently built national sub-corpora, three languages, three legal traditions, and, on the dataset's own account, three different research assistants' worth of manual spot-checking. That pattern is set out jurisdiction by jurisdiction below.

### B. Brazil: Post-Litigation Accommodation and the Zero-Win Settlement

Of Brazil's 11,724 coded decisions, 48.52% concern tariff and billing disputes, a category that presupposes an existing, formal utility contract.[^15] By contrast, only 0.75% of decisions (88 cases) concern informal or irregular settlements, and of those 88 cases, the plaintiff won precisely zero.[^16] Connection refusal disputes, which do not require an existing contract but do typically require a documented application for connection, constitute 10.88% of the corpus (1,275 cases), with a claimant win rate of 17.3%, itself markedly lower than the win rate on the general run of tariff and pipe-damage disputes.[^17] Only 1.93% of decisions invoke human rights or right-to-water framing at all, notwithstanding Brazil's express constitutional guarantee of sanitation access, and that figure falls to 0.0% within the informal settlement category specifically.[^18]

Read together, these figures describe a court system that is highly active on behalf of connected households (adjudicating the price, quality, and continuity of a service they already receive) and almost entirely inactive, and when active, entirely unsuccessful, on behalf of households seeking the connection in the first place from a position of informal tenure. Brazil's dataset author frames this as evidence that Brazilian courts function as the primary post-litigation accommodation forum in the country's Legal Last Mile architecture: administrative gatekeeping is weak enough that a large volume of household-level disputes reach the judiciary, but the population most structurally excluded from formal tenure, precisely the population an accommodation model would need to reach to be meaningful, does not reach the judiciary at a rate remotely proportionate to its likely size, and loses when it does.[^19] This paper's contribution is to note what that means for a hypothetical agentic triage system built on this record: a model trained to predict litigation outcomes, or deployed to flag which eligibility disputes merit escalation, would learn from a training distribution in which the informal settlement claimant is both statistically negligible and, on the rare occasions they appear, a reliable loser. Absent deliberate correction, that is not a neutral starting point.

### C. The Netherlands: Pre-Litigation Absorption

The Dutch sub-corpus tells an almost inverse story, and one that is, on its face, more encouraging. Of 68,654 coded decisions, 98.56% were classified as not substantively water-related after the dataset's residual audit, and of the genuine water-related residual, household connection refusal disputes number just eight, or 0.012% of the total corpus.[^20] The dataset's author attributes this to the Netherlands' universal service obligation, its administrative objections and appeal procedure under the *Algemene wet bestuursrecht* (the *bezwaar* and *beroep* stages), a dedicated consumer disputes tribunal for energy and water (the *Geschillencommissie Energie & Water*), and the National Ombudsman, which together absorb household-level disputes before they reach a court that would be captured in this dataset at all.[^21] A search within the excluded category for the term *aansluitplicht* (connection obligation) returns fourteen decisions, all of them electricity or heat-network disputes under different statutory regimes, and none of them water.[^22] That is read, plausibly, as an affirmative finding: Dutch water connection disputes are not merely rare, they are structurally routed to a different, non-judicial forum entirely.

This is, at first glance, evidence against the Administrative Ghost thesis rather than for it: perhaps a well-designed administrative front end genuinely can absorb household disputes fairly, rather than merely disappearing them. But the dataset's own author flags precisely the limitation that prevents this conclusion from being drawn with confidence, and it is worth stating plainly because it is central to this paper's argument: the Dutch sub-dataset classifies dispute type but does not carry outcome coding.[^23] It is known that Dutch courts do not see many household water connection disputes. It is not known, from this dataset, whether the pre-litigation forums that do see them (the *bezwaar* procedure, the *Geschillencommissie*, the Ombudsman) resolve them in favour of the household or the utility, nor whether households in informal or precarious housing situations (a smaller but real population in Dutch cities) can access those forums at the same rate as documented tenants. A system that absorbs disputes before litigation is not, by that fact alone, a system that resolves them justly. It may equally be a system whose front door is simply better at converting potential claimants into settled or abandoned matters that never generate a written decision of any kind, judicial or otherwise. The Dutch pattern is consistent with genuine inclusion. It is equally consistent with a more efficient, earlier-stage version of the same gatekeeping problem Brazil exhibits later and more visibly. The corpus, as constructed, cannot distinguish between these two possibilities, and this paper does not attempt to resolve that ambiguity from the outside. It notes it because the ambiguity is itself instructive: an administrative system can look, from the vantage point of judicial statistics alone, like a solved problem while remaining, from the vantage point of the excluded household, an unsolved one. An agentic eligibility system audited only against litigation or appeal rates would face exactly this blind spot, and would face it in an aggravated form, because agentic triage happens earlier still, before even the objection stage that the Dutch dataset cannot see behind.

### D. Canada: Fragmented Opacity

The Canadian sub-corpus (3,218 decisions) is dominated by resource governance rather than household service access: fisheries, riparian rights, hydroelectric licensing, and agricultural water allocation together substantially outweigh tariff and connection disputes, which are nearly absent (0.2% and effectively unrepresented respectively).[^24] The dataset's author attributes this to the absence of a federal universal service obligation, the delegation of water and sanitation service delivery to the municipal and utility level, and the correspondingly low visibility of household-level disputes in the reported decisions captured by CanLII, a large share of which (86.9%) are coded from title alone.[^25] Zero decisions are coded as involving Indigenous water rights, a result the dataset's author, correctly in this paper's assessment, treats as an artefact of keyword-based coding on title-only records rather than as evidence that no such disputes exist, given the well-documented scale of water insecurity and administrative contestation affecting First Nations communities in Canada.[^26]

Canada is included here less because its numbers are individually as striking as Brazil's or the Netherlands', and more because it is the clearest illustration of a distinct, third failure mode: opacity by fragmentation. Where Brazil's gatekeeping is visible but unsuccessful for the excluded, and the Netherlands' gatekeeping is largely invisible to this dataset because it happens elsewhere, Canada's gatekeeping is invisible because the relevant record simply is not systematically digitised, indexed, or searchable in a form that any comparative jurimetric method, human or agentic, can currently reach. Unreported decisions from bodies such as the Environmental Review Tribunal and provincial licence appeal tribunals, which the dataset's author flags as a likely source of a disproportionate share of access disputes, are structurally absent from the record on which this paper, the dataset, and any agentic system trained on comparable sources would necessarily rely.[^27] This is a distinct and independently important design lesson for Part V: a Ghost population does not require active gatekeeping to be produced. Institutional fragmentation and incomplete digitisation produce it as a byproduct, and an agentic system's training or retrieval corpus will silently reproduce whatever fragmentation already exists in its sources, with no internal signal that anything is missing.

### E. The Common Pattern

Three jurisdictions, three legal traditions (civil law, civil law, and common law), three administrative philosophies (weak gatekeeping with post-litigation judicial accommodation, strong front-end absorption, and fragmented delegation), and one converging result: the population whose access to an essential service is most precarious is the population least visible, in different ways and for different structural reasons, to the formal legal record that any downstream system, human or agentic, would use to understand how the service access regime actually operates. This is the Administrative Ghost, generalised beyond any one jurisdiction's institutional idiosyncrasies: a structural property of administrative eligibility architecture, not a defect specific to Brazilian courts, Dutch tribunals, or Canadian data infrastructure individually. It is this generalised property, rather than any single jurisdiction's statistics, that Part III argues current automated-decision-making scholarship and regulation are not built to address.

## III. From Discretionary Gatekeeping to Agentic Gatekeeping

### A. What the Literature Already Knows

The legal literature on automated administrative decision-making is now substantial, and it is right about a great deal. Danielle Citron's account of "technological due process" identified, as early as 2008, that automated systems administering public benefits routinely denied claimants the individualised notice, the meaningful opportunity to be heard, and the transparent reasoning that due process doctrine requires, replacing discretionary but contestable human judgment with rule-based but opaque code.[^28] Cary Coglianese and David Lehr's account of machine-learning-era administrative decision-making mapped, with considerable care, where such systems could improve on human discretion (consistency, scale, freedom from certain forms of bias) and where they introduced new risks (opacity, brittleness at the margins, difficulty of judicial review).[^29] Jennifer Cobbe's analysis of judicial review of automated public-sector decisions, and Monika Zalnieriute, Lyria Bennett Moses, and George Williams's analysis of the rule of law implications of government automation, both work through, in careful doctrinal detail, how existing administrative law concepts (reasons, discretion, proportionality, legality) apply, strain, or fail to apply cleanly to an automated decision-maker.[^30] Ryan Calo and Danielle Citron's more recent account of "a crisis of legitimacy" in the automated administrative state broadens the frame further, arguing that automation undermines not just individual due process rights but the deeper legitimacy conditions (accountability, transparency, reason-giving) that make administrative governance defensible at all.[^31] Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* documents, ethnographically rather than doctrinally, how automated eligibility and risk-scoring systems in United States public benefits administration reproduced and, in places, intensified the exclusion of the populations they were nominally built to serve.[^32] Joe Tomlinson's account of digital administrative justice examines how digitisation reshapes the practical accessibility of appeal and review mechanisms for ordinary claimants.[^33]

This is not a small or careless body of work, and this paper's argument should not be read as a claim that the literature has been naive. It is instead a claim about where, collectively, this literature has trained its attention, for good and understandable reasons. Citron's central cases are people denied Medicaid, food assistance, or unemployment benefits by an automated system, after they applied.[^34] Eubanks's central cases are families flagged by predictive risk models after they have already come into contact with a child welfare agency, or applicants for benefits after they have already filed a claim.[^35] Coglianese and Lehr's central examples are regulatory enforcement and permitting decisions made about regulated entities that are, by definition, already within the regulator's jurisdiction and visible to it.[^36] Cobbe's and Zalnieriute, Bennett Moses, and Williams's doctrinal analyses proceed, as administrative law itself generally does, from the premise that there is a decision to review, which in turn presupposes that there was a decision-maker who considered, and therefore was aware of, the applicant.[^37]

### B. The Missing Case: Systems That Screen Before the Claim Exists

The water law dataset surfaces a different and, this paper argues, prior problem. In the Brazilian, Dutch, and Canadian architectures alike, the most excluded households are excluded not principally through an erroneous decision made about their claim, but through a set of prior conditions (a registered address, a utility contract, formal property tenure, documentary evidence of occupancy) that determine whether a claim can be formed and recognised as such at all. Citron's technological due process framework, valuable as it is, protects the claimant against an unfair automated decision on an existing application; it does not, and was not designed to, address a household whose informal tenure means no application is filed, no case file is opened, and no automated or human decision-maker ever considers their circumstances individually enough to be unfair to them. Cobbe's judicial review framework requires a decision amenable to review; there is nothing to review when no decision was made. Coglianese and Lehr's account of regulatory brittleness "at the margins" describes edge cases within a population the regulator already sees; it does not describe a population entirely outside the regulator's field of view. Even Eubanks's more structurally attentive account, which comes closest to this paper's concern, is built substantially around risk-scoring and eligibility-verification systems that act on people who have entered the benefits system, rather than systems, or the absence of systems, that determine who enters it in the first place.

This is not a criticism of the literature so much as an identification of its outer boundary, and a claim that agentic administrative systems are likely to push directly against that boundary rather than staying safely inside it. An agentic system deployed to triage eligibility for a water or sanitation subsidy, to prioritise inspection or connection requests, or to detect fraud or ineligibility in an existing programme, will typically be trained or configured using exactly the kind of administrative record this dataset examines: case files, applications, tariff accounts, connection requests. If that record already structurally under-represents informal-settlement households, relative to their true share of need, then a system trained to predict outcomes, flag anomalies, or allocate scarce administrative attention from that record will, by ordinary statistical logic rather than any malicious design choice, allocate less attention to exactly the population Part II identifies as most excluded. Unlike Citron's or Eubanks's cases, this need not involve the system ever making a wrong decision about anyone. It can occur entirely through the system correctly, faithfully, and transparently reflecting a training distribution that was never representative of true need in the first place. This is a harder problem than an unfair decision, because there is no decision to contest, no notice that was owed and not given, no reasons that were owed and not stated. The doctrinal and regulatory tools examined in Part IV are built almost entirely for the world of decisions. The Ghost lives in the world before decisions.

## IV. Doctrinal Terrain for Agentic Eligibility Systems

### A. Due Process as a Claimant-Side Doctrine

The clearest illustration of the claimant-side orientation identified in Part III is due process doctrine itself. In the United States, the governing framework for assessing what process is due before a deprivation of a benefit or entitlement is the three-factor balancing test of *Mathews v Eldridge*: the private interest affected, the risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used (and the value of additional safeguards), and the government's interest, including administrative burden.[^38] Every element of that test presupposes a person from whom something is, or is about to be, taken, which in turn presupposes that the person was recognised by the administering system as a rights-holder in the first instance. A household that was never recognised as eligible to apply, because it lacked the documentary tenure the eligibility rule required, has no deprivation to weigh under *Mathews*: nothing was taken from it, because nothing was ever extended to it that could be taken away. The doctrine has no native vocabulary for a person who was filtered out at the threshold rather than processed unfairly once inside. This is not a flaw unique to *Mathews*; equivalent claimant-side structures recur across the jurisdictions studied here, from the Brazilian constitutional guarantee of sanitation access, which the dataset shows is invoked in barely one in fifty decisions and never in an informal-settlement case, to the Dutch *Awb* objections procedure, which by definition can only be triggered by someone who has received a decision to object to.[^39]

### B. The EU AI Act's Blind Spot

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act is, on its face, the regulatory instrument best positioned to address exactly the systems this paper is concerned with. Annex III, read with Article 6(2), classifies as high-risk any AI system intended to be used by or on behalf of public authorities to evaluate the eligibility of natural persons for essential public assistance benefits and services, including healthcare, and to grant, reduce, revoke, or reclaim such benefits and services, triggering conformity assessment, risk management, data governance, human oversight, and registration obligations before the system may be placed on the market or put into service.[^40] Water and sanitation eligibility and subsidy triage systems, of the kind already emerging in several of the jurisdictions in this dataset and more widely, would plausibly fall within this category.

The Act's obligations, however, are activated by and framed around the system's evaluation of a person: its risk management and data governance duties concern the data used to train and evaluate the system, and its human oversight duties concern the human's ability to intervene in or override the system's outputs about a given case.[^41] None of these obligations, on their own terms, require a provider or deployer to ask whether the population the system evaluates is itself a representative sample of the population that has a genuine claim to the benefit or service in question. A water subsidy eligibility system trained and validated on historical applicant data, applying rigorous data governance and human oversight to every application it receives, can satisfy the Act's requirements in full while never being asked, let alone required, to detect that an entire category of eligible household never applies at all, for reasons unrelated to the system's own accuracy. The Act regulates how well a system decides among the applications it receives. It does not, on the terms available at the time of writing, regulate whether the applicant pool itself has already been shaped by the same documentary and procedural barriers this paper's empirical evidence traces across three legal systems. This is the AI Act's blind spot, and it is the same blind spot identified in the due process doctrine of Part IV.A, arrived at through an entirely different route, which is itself some evidence that the gap is structural to the claimant-side paradigm rather than an accident of any one instrument's drafting.

### C. Three Institutional Analogues

If claimant-side doctrine and regulation cannot, by construction, reach the Ghost population, the comparative institutional record examined in Part II nonetheless offers three partial, non-doctrinal analogues worth taking seriously as design inputs rather than legal constraints, because each represents a real institution's attempt, imperfect in each case, to see past the claimant-side threshold.

The Dutch *bezwaar* and *beroep* system, together with the *Geschillencommissie* and the Ombudsman, illustrates a front-loaded, multi-channel absorption model: multiple, procedurally lighter-weight forums exist before formal litigation, in principle reducing the documentary and procedural burden of raising a dispute at all.[^42] Its limitation, identified in Part II.C, is that its very success at absorption makes it invisible to exactly the kind of outcome-level scrutiny (including jurimetric analysis of the kind this dataset performs) that would allow anyone outside the system to verify whether it is absorbing disputes justly or merely efficiently.

The Brazilian *Ministério Público*'s role, appearing in 3.6% of the coded corpus and in 13.8% of decisions with an explicit public-interest or collective framing, illustrates a different model: a standing public institution empowered to bring collective claims on behalf of a diffuse or unidentified class of affected persons, without requiring each affected household to individually self-identify as a claimant first.[^43] This is, in institutional miniature, exactly the capability an agentic Ghost-detection function would need: an actor, human or automated, with the mandate and the data access to identify a pattern of exclusion at the population level and act on it, rather than waiting for an individual claim to trigger review.

Canada's fragmentation, examined in Part II.D as a failure mode, is nonetheless instructive precisely because it shows what happens in the absence of either mechanism: no strong front-end absorption system comparable to the Dutch model, and no standing collective-claims institution comparable to the Brazilian *Ministério Público*, leaving household-level water access disputes dispersed across unreported tribunal decisions that are neither judicially visible nor collectively represented. Canada is the baseline against which the other two jurisdictions' partial solutions should be read: not the worst outcome for excluded households necessarily, since fragmentation may in some cases mean simply that resolution happens locally and informally, but the worst-understood outcome, and therefore the hardest starting point from which to design an accountable agentic system, because so little of the record needed to audit such a system for Ghost-detection failure has ever been created.

## V. Ghost-Detection by Design

### A. Four Design Principles

Drawing on the comparative institutional record in Part IV.C and the doctrinal gap identified in Part IV.A and IV.B, this paper proposes four design principles for agentic eligibility systems administering essential services such as water and sanitation. These are offered as design and procedural commitments that could be adopted by a system's deployer, or required by a regulator willing to extend the logic of instruments like the AI Act beyond the claimant-side paradigm, rather than as an exhaustive or final framework.

First, an **affirmative visibility duty**: an agentic eligibility system's deployer should be required to estimate, using independent data (census, informal settlement mapping, utility infrastructure records) rather than the system's own applicant pool, the population plausibly eligible for the service in question, and to report the gap between that estimate and the population the system actually evaluates. This directly targets the failure mode identified in Part III.B, where a system can be statistically faithful to its training data while that data is itself unrepresentative of true need, a failure invisible under both *Mathews*-style due process review and the AI Act's applicant-side data governance obligations.

Second, **standing without documentary priors**: consistent with the Brazilian *Ministério Público* analogue in Part IV.C, at least one channel for triggering review of an agentic eligibility system's exclusionary effect should not require the excluded household itself to have the documentary tenure that its exclusion, ex hypothesi, means it lacks. A collective or public-interest actor, human or an agentic system's own designated auditor, should be able to raise a pattern-level exclusion claim on behalf of an unidentified class, rather than the system's accountability mechanisms depending entirely on individual self-identification.

Third, **auditable negative space**: consistent with the lesson of Part II.C's Dutch case study, where high absorption rates could not be distinguished from good and merely more efficient exclusion using outcome data alone, a system's deployer should be required to retain and make available, in a form auditable by a body independent of the deployer, records not only of the decisions the system made but of the population it never evaluated at all, including reasons where those are known (no application received, application rejected at an earlier automated or human triage stage, application abandoned). Part II.D's Canadian case study shows what happens when this record is not kept: the population most in need of scrutiny becomes, simultaneously, the population least available for it.

Fourth, **independent agentic ombuds-review**: given the discrepancy identified in Part I.9 (three unreconciled reliability figures within a single, relatively small, human-supervised research dataset), this paper does not consider it realistic to rely on a deploying agency's own internal validation of an agentic eligibility system's Ghost-detection performance. An independent reviewing function, analogous to the Dutch National Ombudsman but with an explicit mandate and the technical access needed to audit an agentic system's applicant pool against the affirmative visibility estimate in the first principle, is a necessary complement to the other three principles rather than an optional addition to them.

### B. Objections

Two objections deserve direct engagement. The first is that an affirmative visibility duty risks becoming an unfunded and unfalsifiable mandate: independent population estimates for informal settlements are themselves imperfect, contested, and often as incomplete as the administrative data they are meant to check.[^44] This is a genuine limitation rather than a decisive objection. The comparative record in Part II shows precisely why: Canada's own Indigenous water rights coding gap, and the dataset's honest acknowledgement that a zero-case finding there is almost certainly an artefact of incomplete data rather than an accurate null result, illustrates that even the correcting estimate can itself be a product of the same structural invisibility it is meant to counteract.[^45] The appropriate response is not to abandon the principle but to treat the visibility gap itself, including its own measurement uncertainty, as a reportable and improvable metric over time, in the same way financial and safety regulation treats estimation uncertainty as a disclosed feature of a reporting obligation rather than a reason to have no reporting obligation at all.

The second objection is that these principles, drawn from three specific jurisdictions' water law architecture, may not generalise to other essential services or other legal systems. This paper's answer is that generalisation was never the claim; the claim is narrower and, this author submits, more defensible: that a comparative empirical record spanning three legal traditions and one specific essential service converges on a pattern robust enough, and a doctrinal gap consistent enough across independently developed frameworks (American due process, EU risk regulation, Dutch administrative procedure, Brazilian collective action law), to justify treating Ghost-detection as a distinct design requirement for agentic eligibility systems generally, with the water law record serving as the clearest available evidentiary basis for that requirement rather than as its outer limit.

## VI. Conclusion

The households missing from three national water law caselaws are missing for different proximate reasons: Brazilian informal settlement residents who reach court and lose, Dutch households absorbed into forums this dataset cannot see behind, and Canadian claimants dispersed across an unreported and undigitised tribunal record. What they share is more important than what separates them: none of the standard tools legal scholarship and regulation have built for scrutinising automated administrative decision-making, from technological due process doctrine to the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations, are built to notice a population that was excluded before a decision about it was ever made. An agentic eligibility system trained or deployed on the kind of administrative record this dataset examines inherits that blind spot by default, not through any error in its training or any flaw in its outputs, but through simple and accurate fidelity to a record that was never representative in the first place.

This paper has argued that the correction for this is not better decisions but a different question, asked earlier and by a different actor than the deployer's own internal validation: not "did the system decide this case correctly," but "does the system's applicant pool resemble the true population of need, and who is positioned to find out if it does not." The comparative institutional record examined here, imperfectly in every jurisdiction studied, has already generated partial answers to that question: the Dutch multi-channel absorption model, the Brazilian collective-claims institution, and, by cautionary example, the Canadian record of what happens when neither exists. None of the three is sufficient alone. Read together, and read specifically for what an agentic administrative system would need to reproduce their strengths and avoid their shared weakness, they sketch a design obligation this paper has called Ghost-Detection by Design: an affirmative duty to look for the population the system was never shown, before that population's absence becomes, once again, permanent, machine-readable, and mistaken for consent.
